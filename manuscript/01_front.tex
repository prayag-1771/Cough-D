\title[Prompted labels and easy negatives inflate cough detection accuracy]{Prompted
labels and easy negatives inflate cough detection accuracy: a controlled comparison
across three label regimes}

\author[1]{\fnm{Prayag} \sur{Sharma}}\email{prayag.sharma2024@vitstudent.ac.in}

\author*[2]{\fnm{Ravi} \sur{Tiwari}}\email{ravi.tiwari@vit.ac.in}

\abstract{Reported accuracies for automatic cough detection sit in the high nineties, yet
audits report that such models are seldom validated outside their development corpus and
often separate the classes by a confound. Which part is responsible has not been isolated,
because label provenance, negative class difficulty and run to run variation ordinarily
move together. This study holds the preprocessing, the architecture and the training
protocol fixed and varies the provenance of the label. Three corpora are used, labelled
respectively by the instruction a participant followed, by a human listener against an
audio ontology, and by the score of another classifier. The class prior is matched across
all three, partitions are speaker disjoint, every condition is repeated under five seeds,
and conditions are compared by McNemar's test on paired decisions. Within the prompted
corpus a ResNet18 reaches 98.6 per cent balanced accuracy on held out speakers. The same
weights, applied without retraining to human annotated audio at the same prior, reach 70.2
per cent: specificity falls to 84.3 per cent while sensitivity falls to 56.0. The loss is
one of discrimination, not calibration, the area under the curve falling from 0.998 to
0.749, so moving the threshold recovers little of it. Sneezes are assigned to the cough
class more often than coughs are, locating the failure in a negative class that holds no
forced expirations. Wavelet shrinkage does not help, removing a median of 0.67 per cent
of signal power. The external set is small and single source, so the fall is estimated
less precisely in size than in direction.}

\keywords{Audio classification, External validation, Wavelet shrinkage, Respiratory sound
analysis, Dataset bias}
